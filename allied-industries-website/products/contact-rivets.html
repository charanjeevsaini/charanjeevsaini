Contact Rivets

Electrical contact rivets, cold-headed for a soft shank and a hardened head.

Manufactured with wire by cold heading: the shank stays soft for easy riveting while the head work-hardens. Shank length can be varied; shank diameter is held in proportion to head size to avoid wrinkling during cold forging.

Contact rivet types we manufacture

Trimetal Contact Rivets

An economical alternative to solid silver contacts, without compromising on quality. We manufacture Trimetal Contact Rivets with an AgCdO / AgNi / Ag999 layer bonded to ETP Copper on both sides.

AgCdOAgNiAg999

Weldable Button Contact Rivets

Projection-weldable contacts with an AgCdO / AgNi layer on a Nickel, CuNi or Steel base — built to fuse directly onto your terminal or busbar.

AgCdOAgNiNi / CuNi / Steel base

Bimetal Contact Rivets

The contact layer is normally half the thickness of the head; the ratio can be varied within practical limits to suit specific requirements. Contact layer available in AgCdO, AgNi, AgSnO, Ag999 and other silver alloys.

AgCdOAgNiAgSnOAg999

Disc Contact Rivets

Disc-type contacts available in all alloy compositions as per customer specification — sized to the switching current and mechanical mounting your part requires.

Technical data

Contact alloys, backings and the sizes they come in

Choosing a contact alloy is a trade between erosion resistance, welding behaviour and contact resistance. Here is how the common ones compare.

The facing carries the arc. This is where the switching performance of the part is decided.

AgCdO — silver cadmium oxide

The long-established switching alloy: the cadmium oxide disperses arc energy and keeps the contact from welding shut.

Advantages

  • Outstanding resistance to arc erosion
  • Very low tendency to weld under inrush
  • Contact resistance stays stable over many operations

Trade-offs

  • Cadmium is restricted under RoHS; use depends on the exemption that applies to your product
  • Being designed out in favour of AgSnO in most new work
  • Requires controlled handling in manufacture

Typical applications: Relays, contactors and switches in legacy or exemption-covered designs.

AgNi — silver nickel

A RoHS-compliant workhorse where the duty is moderate and low contact resistance matters.

Advantages

  • Free of restricted substances
  • Low contact resistance, close to fine silver
  • Cold forms and machines well, so tight head profiles are practical

Trade-offs

  • More prone to welding than AgCdO at high inrush
  • Erosion resistance falls away at the top of the current range
  • Nickel content can oxidise at elevated temperature

Typical applications: Appliance and automotive switches, general-purpose relays.

AgSnO — silver tin oxide

The mainstream RoHS replacement for AgCdO, and the usual choice for high-inrush duty.

Advantages

  • Excellent anti-weld performance under heavy inrush
  • Erosion resistance comparable to AgCdO
  • Contains no restricted substances

Trade-offs

  • Higher contact resistance, so the contact runs hotter
  • Harder to cold form than AgNi, which constrains head geometry
  • Generally the more expensive option

Typical applications: Contactors, automotive relays, motor and lamp loads with high inrush.

Ag999 — fine silver

Unalloyed silver, specified when contact resistance must be as low as physically possible.

Advantages

  • Lowest contact resistance and highest conductivity available
  • Very ductile, so it clads and forms easily
  • Predictable, well-characterised behaviour

Trade-offs

  • Soft — it deforms and wears under repeated mechanical action
  • Welds readily, so it is unsuited to switching inductive loads
  • Tarnishes in sulphur-bearing atmospheres

Typical applications: Low-current signal contacts, thermostats, sensing and instrument contacts.

Behind the facing, the base metal carries current away and provides the mechanical joint.

ETP Copper (C11000)

The default base for electrical work — around 100% IACS conductivity and ductile enough to cold head without annealing.

Advantages

  • Highest conductivity of the common base metals
  • Very ductile, so the shank sets cleanly without cracking
  • Naturally corrosion resistant in dry indoor service

Trade-offs

  • Low shear strength compared with steel
  • Work hardens, so heavy forming may need an interstage anneal
  • Higher material cost than steel or aluminium

Typical applications: Wiring harness terminals, earth and bonding connections, busbar joints.

Brass (CuZn)

A copper-zinc alloy that trades some conductivity for strength and much better machinability.

Advantages

  • Stronger and harder than pure copper
  • Machines and trims cleanly to a burr-free edge
  • Holds a bright finish without plating

Trade-offs

  • Roughly a quarter to a third of copper's conductivity
  • Susceptible to dezincification in damp or saline service
  • Stress-corrosion cracking risk if left with forming stresses

Typical applications: Switchgear hardware, terminal blocks, fasteners where appearance matters.

Nickel / CuNi

A backing and barrier material where heat, oxidation or weldability drive the choice.

Advantages

  • Resists oxidation at elevated temperature
  • Projection-welds reliably to terminals and busbars
  • Acts as a diffusion barrier under silver

Trade-offs

  • Markedly more expensive than copper or brass
  • Lower conductivity than copper
  • Pure nickel is ferromagnetic, which matters in some sensing applications

Typical applications: Weldable contact backings, high-temperature and harsh-environment parts.
