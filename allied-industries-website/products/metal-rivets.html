Metal Rivets

Semi Tubular Rivets

Trimmed Rivets

Materials

Built from the alloy your application calls for

Every rivet is cold-headed directly from wire stock — a process that keeps the shank soft for easy riveting while the head is work-hardened for strength. Shank length can be varied to your drawing; shank diameter is held in the right proportion to head size to avoid wrinkling during cold forging.

Technical data

Materials, finishes and the sizes they come in

What each base metal and plating actually buys you, and where it costs you — so you can pick before you send a drawing.

Every rivet starts as drawn wire. The base metal sets conductivity, strength and how much forming the part will take.

ETP Copper (C11000)

The default base for electrical work — around 100% IACS conductivity and ductile enough to cold head without annealing.

Advantages

  • Highest conductivity of the common base metals
  • Very ductile, so the shank sets cleanly without cracking
  • Naturally corrosion resistant in dry indoor service

Trade-offs

  • Low shear strength compared with steel
  • Work hardens, so heavy forming may need an interstage anneal
  • Higher material cost than steel or aluminium

Typical applications: Wiring harness terminals, earth and bonding connections, busbar joints.

Brass (CuZn)

A copper-zinc alloy that trades some conductivity for strength and much better machinability.

Advantages

  • Stronger and harder than pure copper
  • Machines and trims cleanly to a burr-free edge
  • Holds a bright finish without plating

Trade-offs

  • Roughly a quarter to a third of copper's conductivity
  • Susceptible to dezincification in damp or saline service
  • Stress-corrosion cracking risk if left with forming stresses

Typical applications: Switchgear hardware, terminal blocks, fasteners where appearance matters.

Mild steel

The structural choice: cheap and strong, but it must be plated for any corrosion or contact duty.

Advantages

  • Highest strength per unit cost
  • Takes tin, nickel or zinc plating readily
  • Dimensionally stable under load

Trade-offs

  • Poor electrical conductivity — not a contact material
  • Will rust quickly if the plating is breached
  • Less ductile, so head profiles are more limited

Typical applications: Structural riveting, brake and clutch linings, general mechanical assembly.

Aluminium

Chosen where weight matters more than strength, and where the joint stays dry.

Advantages

  • Roughly a third the density of copper
  • Self-passivating oxide resists corrosion
  • Very soft — sets with low setting force

Trade-offs

  • Low shear and tensile strength
  • Galvanic corrosion when coupled to copper or steel in damp service
  • Oxide film raises contact resistance unless broken by the set

Typical applications: Lightweight assemblies, nameplates, ducting and trim.

Nickel / CuNi

A backing and barrier material where heat, oxidation or weldability drive the choice.

Advantages

  • Resists oxidation at elevated temperature
  • Projection-welds reliably to terminals and busbars
  • Acts as a diffusion barrier under silver

Trade-offs

  • Markedly more expensive than copper or brass
  • Lower conductivity than copper
  • Pure nickel is ferromagnetic, which matters in some sensing applications

Typical applications: Weldable contact backings, high-temperature and harsh-environment parts.

Plating decides how the joint behaves after a year in service, not on the day it is set.

Tin

The general-purpose electrical finish: cheap, solderable and low in contact resistance when fresh.

Advantages

  • Excellent solderability
  • Low and stable contact resistance at low voltage
  • Inexpensive and widely specified

Trade-offs

  • Can grow whiskers over time in thin bright deposits
  • Softens and creeps under sustained contact pressure
  • Service temperature limited by tin's low melting point

Typical applications: Connectors, crimp terminals, PCB hardware.

Nickel

Usually an underplate rather than a final finish — it stops copper migrating into the top layer.

Advantages

  • Hard and wear resistant under repeated mating
  • Effective diffusion barrier beneath silver or gold
  • Holds up at elevated temperature

Trade-offs

  • Higher contact resistance than tin, silver or gold
  • Passive oxide film needs wiping action to break through
  • Ferromagnetic in its pure form

Typical applications: Underplate below silver, high-temperature parts, wear surfaces.

Silver

The highest-performing contact finish, used where current or contact resistance is the constraint.

Advantages

  • Lowest electrical contact resistance of the practical finishes
  • Highest thermal and electrical conductivity
  • Good anti-stick behaviour on make and break

Trade-offs

  • Tarnishes to silver sulphide in polluted air
  • Soft, so it wears under repeated mechanical wipe
  • The most expensive of the common finishes

Typical applications: Switching contacts, high-current terminals, relay and contactor faces.

Zinc

A sacrificial coating for steel parts that carry load rather than current.

Advantages

  • Protects steel galvanically even where scratched
  • Very low cost per part
  • Chromate or passivate topcoats extend its life considerably

Trade-offs

  • Not suitable as an electrical contact surface
  • Forms white corrosion product in humid storage
  • Hydrogen embrittlement risk on higher-strength steels

Typical applications: Structural steel rivets, non-electrical mechanical fasteners.
